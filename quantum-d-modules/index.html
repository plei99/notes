Abstract.

In this lecture series, we review the basics of quantum D-modules and Givental’s formalism. We then explain the projective bundle formula and the blowup formula, which are proved using Fourier transforms associated with rank-one torus actions. If time permits, we will also discuss a nonabelian version of the Fourier transform.

1 GW theory

One motivation for us comes from mirror symmetry. Let X be a Fano variety (or more generally, log Calabi-Yau). Then we expect there to be a mirror Xˇ, which is an LG model. Via some version of Saito theory (which may not actually be well-defined), we obtain a Gauss-Manin conjecture. This will correspond to the quantum D-module of X, which is defined using Gromov-Witten theory.

Another motivation comes from the study of other areas of algebraic geometry1. Katzarkov-Kontsevich-Pantev-Yu define Hodge atoms which establish an irrationality criterion for varieties and was used to prove the irrationality of the cubic fourfold. Recently, an unreleased internal model at OpenAI proved the generalized Mukai conjecture using Gromov-Witten theory.

1.1 Quantum D-modules.

Let X be a smooth projective variety and (ϕi)i be a homogeneous basis with corresponding coordinates (τi)i. Then we define the (big) quantum cohomology

QH∗(X)≔(H∗(X)⟦Q,τ⟧,⋆),

where ℂ⁡[Q]≔ℂ⁡[NEℕ⁢(X)] keeps track of effective curve classes, and here we are taking a graded completion of ℂ⁡[Q,τ] where

deg⁡Qβ=2⁢∫βc1⁢(X)anddeg⁡τi=2−deg⁡ϕi.
Example 1.1.

Let X=ℙn and τ=0. Then the curve classes are just non-negative integers, so Q has degree 2⁢(n+1). In this case, ℂ⁢⟦Q⟧=ℂ⁡[Q].

Example 1.2.

Let X have ℂ⁡[Q]=ℂ⁡[Q1,Q2] where deg⁡Q1=2 and deg⁡Q2=−2. In this case, we actually have

ℂ⟦Q1,Q2⟧=⨁n≥0Q1nℂ⟦Q1Q2⟧⊕⨁n<0Q2nℂ⟦Q1Q2⟧,

which lives in all even degrees.

Now let α,β,γ∈H∗⁢(X). The quantum product is defined by the formula

(α⋆β,γ)X≔∑d,nQdn!⁢⟨α,β,γ,τ,…,n⟩0,n+3,dX,

where (−,−)X is the Poincaré pairing and τ=∑iτi⁢ϕi.

Remark 1.3.

We can define the quantum cohomology as a formal Frobenius manifold, but this is equivalent to the approach taken by the speaker, so we will skip it.

Definition 1.4.

We will now define the quantum D-module

QDM(X)≔(H∗(X)[z]⟦Q,τ⟧,∇X,PX).

The connection ∇X is known as the quantum connection and is defined by

∇z⁢ξ⁢Q⁢∂QX ≔zξQ∂Q+ξ⋆−,
∇z⁢∂τiX ≔z∂τi+ϕi⋆−
∇z2⁢∂zX ≔z2∂z−(EX⋆−)+z⋅μX.

Here, the first definition makes sense only for ξ∈H2⁢(X) and the Euler vector field is defined by

EX≔c1⁢(X)+∑i(1−deg⁡ϕi2)⁢τi⁢ϕi.

Additionally, the grading operator is defined by μX⁢(ϕi)=deg⁡ϕi−dimX2⁢ϕi. Finally, the pairing

PX≔QDM(X)⊗2→ℂ[z]⟦Q,τ⟧

is given by the formula

PX⁢(f⁡(z),g⁡(z))≔∫Xf⁡(−z)∪g⁡(z).
Example 1.5.

Let X=ℙ1. Then we have

QH∗⁡(ℙ1)≅ℂ⁡[p,Q,τ0]⁢⟦τ1⟧p2−Q⁢eτ1

and

QDM⁡(ℙ1)=H∗⁢(ℙ1)⁢[z,Q,τ0]⁢⟦τ1⟧

with the relevant connections.

Remarks 1.6.
  1. (1)

    The object QDM⁡(X) is not a D-module in the usual sense of D-modules.2

  2. (2)

    The objects QH and QDM contain the same information, namely that one can be recovered from the other.

  3. (3)

    The ring QH∗⁡(X) is a quantization of the usual cohomology ring H∗⁢(X) as a ring and QDM⁡(X) is a quantization of H∗⁢(X) as a graded H∗⁢(X)-module.

We will now produce the fundamental solution MX⁢(τ) to the quantum connection. This is given by the formula

(MX⁢(τ)⁢α,β)X=(α,β)X+∑(n,d≠(0,0))Qdn!⁢⟨βz−ψ,τ,…,τ,α⟩0,n+2,dX

for all α,β∈H∗⁢(X).

Proposition 1.7.

We have

|z⁢ξ⁢Q⁢∂Q+ξ∪z⁢∂τiz2⁢∂z−c1⁢(X)+z⋅μX|∘MX⁢(τ)=MX⁢(τ)∘|zξQ∂Q+ξ⋆z∂τi+ϕi⋆z2∂z−EX⋆+z⋅μX|.

Moreover, MX⁢(τ)⁢1=JX⁢(τ) and MX⁢(τ)⁢ϕi=z⁢∂τiJX⁢(τ). We also have the asymptotics

MX⁢(τ)=eτ/z⁢id+O⁡(Q)

and the property

(MX⁢(τ)⁢α,MX⁢(τ)|z↦−z⁢β)X=(α,β)X.

1.2 Givental formalism.

Define

𝐭⁡(z)=∑k≥0∑itki⁢ϕi⁢zk

and the genus zero descendent GW potential

ℱX⁢(z⁢1+𝐭⁡(z))≔∑n,dQdn!⁢⟨𝐭⁡(−ψ),…,𝐭⁡(−ψ)⟩0,n,dX.

Now define the symplectic vector space

ℋX≔H∗⁢(X)⁢[z±]⁢⟦Q⟧.

This is actually functorial, but we will skip the discussion of this. Then define the form

ΩX:ℋX×ℋX→ℂ⁡⟦Q⟧

by the formula

ΩX⁢(f⁡(z),g⁡(z))≔[z−1]⁢(f⁡(−z),g⁡(z))X.

This makes ℋX an infinite-dimensional symplectic vector space over ℂ⁢⟦Q⟧.

There is a decomposition

ℋX=ℋ+⊕ℋ−,

where

ℋ+=H∗⁢(X)⁢[z]⁢⟦Q⟧andℋ−=z−1⁢H∗⁢(X)⁢[z−1]⁢⟦Q⟧.

Finally, we can define ℒX to be the graph of dℱX and refer to it as the Givental cone.

Theorem 1.8.

ℒX⊂[H]X is an overruled Lagrangian cone; namely:

  1. (1)

    ℒX⊂(ℋX,ΩX) is Lagrangian;

  2. (2)

    It is a cone, namely that if I⁡(τ)∈ℒX and F⁡(τ,z)⁢I⁡(τ)∈ℒX if F⁡(τ,z)=1+O⁡(τ);

  3. (3)

    For any f∈ℒX, we have Tf⁢ℒX∩ℒX=z⁢Tf⁢ℒX.

Example 1.9.

When X=ℙ1, we see that the basis 1,p,z,p⁢z,z2,… is dual to the basis z−1⁢p,z−1,z−2⁢p,z−2,z−3⁢p,…. When we multiply by z, we see that ℋ− is not preserved.

Remark 1.10.

Let F be a formal function on ℋ+. Then the graph ℒ of dF is an overruled Lagrangian cone if and only if F satisfies the string equation, dilaton equation, and topological recursion relations.

There is an informal description of the Lagrangian cone as

ℒX=⋃t∈H∗⁢(X)⁢⟦Q⟧z⁢Tt⁢ℒX≔z⁢MX⁢(t)⁢ℋ+.

In particular, we see that

MX⁢(τ):QDM⁡(X)→Tτ⁢ℒX

is an isomorphism intertwining ∇ and d. This essentially explains all of genus zero Gromov-Witten theory.

2 Equivariant GW theory

Now let X be a smooth semiprojective (projective over affine) T-variety, where T=(ℂ×)r is a torus. We can run the same formalism as before to obtain

MX,T⁢(τ):QDMT⁡(X)→∼Tτ⁢ℒX,T.

This still intertwines ∇ and d, but there are additional operators, called shift operators, which we will denote by 𝕊 and 𝒮.

In this setting, we have the genus-zero potential

FX,T0⁢(X)≔∑n,dQdn!⁢⟨τ,…,τ⟩0,n,dX,T

and therefore ⋆τ, ∇z⁢∂τi, ∇z⁢ξ⁢Q⁢∂Q, and ∇z2⁢∂z. Here, we will denote by τ=(τi)i∈I coordinates corresponding to a homogeneous basis ϕi)i∈I of H∗⁢(X) and τ=(τi,ℓ→)i∈I,ℓ→∈ℤ≥0r coordinates corresponding to a basis (ϕi⁢λℓ→)i,ℓ→ of HT∗⁢(X)≅H∗⁢(X)⊗HT∗⁢(pt) (here this is a non-canonical isomorphism of HT∗⁢(pt)-modules due to Goresky-Kottwitz-MacPherson).

Note here that we need to make the modification

∇z2⁢∂zX=z2∂z−(EX,T⋆−)+z⋅μX,T

and define

EX,T=c1T⁢(X)+∑i,ℓ→(1−deg⁡ϕi⁢λℓ→2)⁢ϕi⁢τi,ℓ→⁢λℓ

and the operator

μX,T∈Endℂ⁡(HT∗⁢(X)):ϕi⁢λℓ→↦(deg⁡ϕi⁢λℓ→−dimX)⁢ϕi⁢λℓ→.

We also can define

ℱX,T⁢(z+𝐭⁡(z))=∑n,dQdn!⁢⟨𝐭⁡(−ψ),…,𝐭⁡(−ψ)⟩0,n,dX,T

and the Givental cone ℒX,T⊂ℋX,T≔HT∗⁢(X)⁢((z−1))∩HT⁢t⁢i⁢m⁢e⁢s⁢ℂz×∗⁢(X)loc. Note here that the localization occurs in the category of graded rings, so expressions of the form ⟨ϕiz+ψ,…⟩ live in HT×ℂ×∗⁢(pt)loc.

2.1 Shift operators.

Example 2.1.

Let X=ℂn carry the scaling action of ℂ×. Note that τ0 has degree 2, so

QHℂ×∗⁡(ℂn)=ℂ⁡[τ0,λ]

and

QDMℂ×(ℂn)=(ℂ[z,λ]⟦τ0,k;k≥0⟧,∇,𝕊,P).

Since the classical and quantum products coincide, ∇ and P are easy to define. The operator 𝕊 is an endomorphism of ℂ⁡[z,λ]⁢⟦τ⟧ given by the formula

f⁡(z,λ,τ)↦λn⁢f⁢(z,λ−z,0)+O⁡(τ).

Note that Mℂn,ℂ×⁢(τ)=exp⁡(τ/z) gives an isomorphism QDMℂ×⁡(ℂn)≃Tτ⁢ℒℂn,ℂ×, and 𝕊=M−1∘𝒮∘M. In this setting, 𝒮 is given by

g↦λn⁢g|λ↦λ−z,

so the terms containing τ come from M.

Remark 2.2.

From the example we can see that QH and ∇z⁢∂τ do not depend on n, but 𝕊 does.

Remark 2.3.

Later, we will use shift operators to lift Kirwan surjectivity

Hℂ×∗⁢(ℂn)↠H∗⁢(ℙn−1)

to obtain an isomorphism

QDMℂ×⁡(ℂn)→∼s∗⁢QDM⁡(ℙn−1).

More generally, we expect that the “completion”

QDMT⁡(X)L→QDM⁡(X⫽LT)

is a quantization of the usual Kirwan map κL and that QDMT⁡(X) is a Kähler moduli space. with various limit points being GIT quotients X⫽LT or X⫽L′T.

In the example, if we consider small quantum cohomology, we see that

ℂ⁡[λ]≅SQHℂ×∗⁡(ℂn)→∼SQH∗⁡(ℙn−1)≅ℂ⁡[p].

Dependence on the dimension can be recovered by considering the structure as a ℂ⁡[Q]-module.

Remark 2.4.

In symplectic topology, we can study quantum cohomology using the loop space

L⁢X≔{f:S1→X}.

From the torus action on X, we obtain a torus action on L⁢X given by

(t⋅f)⁢(θ)=t⋅f⁡(θ).

There is also an action of π1⁢(T) on L⁢X, where if g:S1→T is a group homomorphism, then

(g∘f)⁢(θ)≔g⁡(θ)⋅f⁡(θ).

This action of π1⁢(T) is where the shift operators come from.

We will now actually introduce shift operators, which defines a ℂ⁡⟦NEℕT⁡(X)⟧-module structure on QDMT⁡(X), where NEℕT⁡(X) is an equivariant analogue of the monoid NEℕ⁡(X) of effective curve classes.

3 Fourier transforms

There are two kinds of Fourier transforms, which were introduced by Iritani: continuous and discrete. Let W be a smooth semi-projective T-variety, where T=ℂ×.

3.1 Discrete Fourier transform.

This is a quantization of the Kirwan map. Let L be a T-linearized ample line bundle on W such that ∅≠Ws=Wss is acted on freely by T and W⫽LT is smooth and projective. Then there is the Kirwan map

κL:HT∗⁢(W)↠H∗⁢(W⫽LT).

Conjecturally, this should quantize to

FTL:QDMT⁡(W)L→QDM⁡(W⫽LT).

It will intertwine the shift operator 𝕊 with the Novikov variable q and the equivariant parameter with the quantum connection ∇z⁢κ⁢(λ)⁢q⁢∂q.

Example 3.1.

Let W=ℂn with the diagonal action. Then the Kirwan map is

ℂ⁡[λ]=HT∗⁢(ℂn)↠H∗⁢(ℙn−1)≅ℂ⁡[p]/(pn)κ⁡(λ)=p.

At the level of small quantum D-modules, we have

SQDMℂ×⁡(ℂn)=Hℂ×⁢(ℂn)⁢[z]=ℂ⁡[λ,z]

but we have a nontrivial shift operator

𝕊:f⁡(λ,z)↦λn⁢f⁢(λ−z,z).

In the quotient, we have

SQDM(ℙn−1)=H∗(ℙn−1)[z,q]∇z⁢q⁢∂q=zq∂q+p⋆0−,

where SQH∗⁡(ℙn−1)=ℂ⁡[p,q]/(pn−q). Note here that SQDM⁡(ℙn−1)=⨁i=0n−1ℂ⁡[z,q]⋅pi whereas SQDMℂ×⁡(ℂn)=⨁i=0n−1ℂ⁡[z,𝕊]⋅λi. Note here that

λn+1=𝕊⁢λ+z⁢𝕊⁢1.

We may now define the map

FT:SQDMℂ×⁡(ℂn)→SQDM⁡(ℙn−1)λi↦pi.

More generally, we have

FT⁡(∑i=1n−1ci⁢(z,𝕊)⁢λi)≔∑i=0n−1ci⁢(z,q)⁢pi.

In this case, FT is an isomorphism. It is clear that it intertwines 𝕊 and q. It is a bit less obvious that it intertwines λ and ∇z⁢q⁢∂q, but this is also not hard to check.

Note that QDMT(W)=HT∗(W)[q]⟦𝒬,𝕌⟧ has the action of 𝕊β for all β∈NEℕT⁡(W), whereas QDMT(W)loc=HT∗(W)loc[z]⟦𝒬,𝕌⟧ has the action of β∈N1T⁢(W).

Let cL⊂cT⁢(W)⊆NT1⁢(W)ℝ be the GIT chamber. Then we define

cL,ℕ∨≔cL∨∩N1T⁢(W)⊆N1T⁢(W).

In progress work of Iritani-Sanda will show that cT⁢(W)∨=NEℕT⁡(W).

Now we can define QDMT⁡(W)L to be the graded completion of

ℂ⁡[cL,ℕ∨]⋅QDMT⁡(W)⊆QDMT⁡(W)loc.

Here, note that deg⁡𝕊β≔2⁢(c1T⁢(W),β).

On the other hand, we see that 𝒮⁢1=λn is killed by the Kirwan map, so in fact we see that

(1)∧ =∑k∈ℤqk⁢κ⁢(𝒮−k⁢1)
=∑k≥0qk⁢1∏c=1k(p+c⁢z)n
=Jℙn−1⁢(τ=0)∈T0⁢ℒℙn−1.

To recover the big I-function, just replace 1 by e𝕌/z.

Theorem 3.5.

The reduction conjecture is true for:

  • •

    Projective bundles ℙ⁡(V)=V⫽ℂ×, due to Iritani-Koto;

  • •

    (Possibly non-split) toric bundles ⨁iVi⫽T, due to Koto;

  • •

    When T=ℂ× and there exists a ℂ×-fixed divisor such that T acts on ND/W with weight ±1 and W⫽Lℂ×=D, due to Iritani. This plays a crucial role in the blowup formula.

3.2 Continuous Fourier transform.

This is a quantization of equivariant localization. Let WT⊇F be a connected component of the fixed locus. Then there exists

FTF:QDMT⁡(W)→QDM⁡(F)⊕cF,

where cF∈ℤ≥0 is determined the action of T on NF/W. Here, it is defined as a phase asymptotic approximation of the integral

∫eη⁡(λ)/z⁢GF′⁢f|F⁢dλ.

Informally, define

GF≔∏ρ: Chern roots of ⁢NF/W(−z)−ρ/z−2⁢π⁢z⁢Γ⁢(−ρz).

Now consider the map

ℋW,T→ℋFf↦gF⋅f|F.

This intertwines 𝕊β with Qβ+σ0⁢(−β¯)⋅e−z⁢β¯⁢∂λ. Applying the classical idea of the Mellin transform, we can consider the integral

∫eλ⁢log⁡sF⁢GF⋅f|F⁢dλ

and turn the shift operator into a variable Sβ. Unfortunately, this integral is ill-defined because the integrand contains log⁡z and log⁡sF.

Instead, we will consider a stationary phase approximation. Here, SF≔SσF⁢(1), where σF:B⁢G→XT is a section. Then, we can rewrite the integral as

∫eλ⁢log⁡SF/z⁢GF⁢f|F⁢dλ=∫eη⁡(λ)/z⁢GF′⁢f|F⁢dλ,

where

η⁡(λ) ≔λ⁢log⁡SF−∑α∈Hom⁡(T,ℂ×)rα⁢(wα⁢λ⁢log⁡(wα⁢λ)−wα⁢λ).

Here, if we write

NF/W=⨁α∈Hom⁡(T,ℂ×)Nα,

then rα≔rk⁡(Nα) and wα≔αλ. Finally, we define

GF′≔GF⋅e∑αrα⁢(wα⁢λ⁢log⁡wα⁢λ−wα⁢λ)/z.

Setting cF≔|∑αrα⁢wα|∈ℤ≥0 and λ0≔SF1/cF (up to scaling by a rational number), then we have the stationary phase approximation

2⁢π⁢z⁢eη⁡(λ0)/z=∏α(wα⁢λ0)−c1⁢(Nα)/z⋅ℱF,0⁢(f).

Note here that η′⁢(λ0)=0.

Definition 3.6.

We define

For all 0≤j≤cF−1 there exists σj:HT∗⁢(W)→H∗⁢(F) and a Fourier transform FTF,j fitting into a commutative diagram

which intertwines 𝕊β and 𝒮β on the left with formal variables Sβ on the right and intertwines λ on the left with ∇z⁢λ⁢S⁢∂S+λj and z⁢λ⁢S⁢∂S+λj on the right. Here, the λj are the critical points of η⁡(λ).

4 Projective bundle formula

Let W≔V be a vector bundle of rank r over a smooth projective base B. Assume that there exists a surjection 𝒪B⊕N↠V∨, which ensures that V is semi-projective. If this is not true, we can replace V by V⊗𝒪B⁢(−M) for some M. Then we see that

V⫽LT≅ℙ⁡(V)

for some L and VT=B.

The reduction conjecture predicts the existence of a Fourier transform

FT:QDMℂ×⁡(V)⇢τ∗⁢QDM⁡(ℙ⁡(V)).

There is also a continuous Fourier transform

FTB:QDMℂ×⁡(V)→⨁i=0r−1σi∗⁢QDM⁡(B).

We will write ℂ⁡⟦Q⟧≔ℂ⁡⟦NEℕ⁡(B)⟧ and note that

QDM(B)=H∗(B)[z]⟦Q,σ⟧

where σ∈H∗⁢(B). We note that

ℂ⁡⟦NEℕ⁡(ℙ⁡(V))⟧=ℂ⁡[q]⁢⟦Q⟧.

Here, β∈NEℕ⁡(ℙ⁡(V)) is associated to q(β,𝒪ℙ⁡(V)⁢(1))⁢Qπ∗⁢β, where π:ℙ⁡(V)→B is the structure morphism.

Theorem 4.1 (Iritani-Koto).

There exists

σi(τ)∈H∗(B)((q−1/r′))⟦Q,τ⟧

for i=0,…,r−1 such that there exists

Φ:QDM⁡(ℙ⁡(V))loc→∼⨁i=0r−1σi∗⁢QDM⁡(B).

Here, QDM(ℙ(V))loc=QDM(ℙ(V))⊗ℂ[z]((q−1/r′))⟦Q,τ⟧. Moreover, Φ intertwines the quantum connections and is linear over ℂ[z]((q−1/r′))⟦Q,τ⟧.

Sketch of proof.

The first step is to prove the reduction conjecture for ℙ⁡(V)=V⫽ℂ×. The second step is to prove that FTL is an isomorphism. Once we have done this, we define a ℂ[z,q]⟦Q,τ⟧-module homomorphism

Φ:QDM⁡(ℙ⁡(V))→⨁i=1r−1σi∗⁢QDM⁡(B)

as

τ∗⁢QDM⁡(ℙ⁡(V))→∼FTL−1QDMℂ×⁡(V)→FTB⨁iσi∗⁢QDM⁡(B).

Unfortunately, this is linear only over ℂ[z,q]⟦Q,𝕌⟧, so we need a lift u:H∗⁢(ℙ⁡(V))→Hℂ×∗⁢(V) (here, u⁡(τ)∈Hℂ×∗⁢(V)⁢⟦τ⟧ and τ⁢(u⁡(τ))=τ) of τ:Hℂ×∗⁢(V)→H∗⁢(ℙ⁡(V)). This is not hard to find, so we obtain a morphism

QDM⁡(ℙ⁡(V))→⨁iσ¯i∗⁢QDM⁡(B),

where σ¯i=σi∘u. Now if we set p=c1⁢(𝒪ℙ⁡(V)⁢(1)) and consider ϕ∈H∗⁢(B), we see that

Φi⁢(ϕ⁢pk)|Q=τ=0=1r⁢λik−r−12⁢(ϕ+O⁡(q−1/r)),

where λi=ζri⁢q1/r. Once we invert q1/r, we see that Φ is an isomorphism. □

Remark 4.2.

For example, when B is a point, then z𝕀(𝕌)=M{r−1(τ(𝕌))⋅R (here the big I-function is defined using light markings) via Birkhoff factorization. This defines τ⁡(𝕌) as a mirror map.

Corollary 4.3.

As rings, we have an isomorphism

(QH∗⁡(ℙ⁡(V)),⋆τ)≅⨁i=1r(QH∗⁡(B),⋆σi⁢(τ))

over ℂ⁡((q−1/r))⁢⟦Q⟧. Moreover, the genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants of ℙ⁡(V) can be recovered from the genus-zero invariants of B and the total Chern class c⁡(V).

Theorem 4.4.

We have

Iℙ⁡(V)=∑k≥0qk⋅JV|λ→p+k⁢z∏c=1k∏δ: Chenr roots of ⁢V(p+δ+c⁢z)∈z−1⁢ℒℙ⁡(V),

where JV is the equivariant J-function of V.

Sketch of proof.

Consider the short exact sequence

0→𝒬→𝒪⊕N↠V∨→0.

This gives us an inclusion V↪𝒪⊕N, which descends to

ℙ⁡(V)↪B×ℙN−1,

which is cut out by a regular section of 𝒬⊠𝒪ℙN−1⁢(1). We can now use quantum Lefschetz to relate ℒℙ⁡(V) and ℒB×ℙN−1,(𝒬⊠𝒪⁡(1),eℂ×). Quantum Riemann-Roch relates this to ℒB×ℙN−1, the work of Brown relates ℒB×ℙN−1 and ℒB, and finally quantum Riemann-Roch relates ℒB and ℒV. □

Remark 4.5.

To prove a mirror theorem for general non-split toric bundles, this strategy fails because 𝒬 is not convex in general. However, we can use virtual localization to reduce to the case of projective bundles.

5 Blowup formula

Let W≔BlZ×0⁡(X×ℙ1).

Theorem 5.1.

There exists a map

H∗⁢(BlZ⁡X)→∼H∗⁢(X)⊕H∗⁢(Z)⊕c−1τ~↦(τ⁡(τ~),{sj⁢(τ~)}0≤j≤c−2)

and a map

Ψ:QDM⁡(BlZ⁡X)loc→∼τ∗⁢QDM⁡(X)⊕⨁j=0c−2sj∗⁢QDM⁡(Z).

Here, c is the codimension of Z and the maps are defined over ℂ((q−1/(c−2)))⟦Q,τ~⟧.

Corollary 5.2.

We obtain a corresponding decomposition

(QH∗⁡(BlZ⁡X),⋆τ~)≅(QH∗⁡(X),⋆τ⁡(τ~))⊕j=0c−2(QH∗⁡(Z),⋆sj⁢(τ~))

over ℂ⁡((q−1/(c−2)))⁢⟦Q⟧.

Theorem 5.3.

The genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants of BlZ⁡X can be recovered from those of X and Z, and from c⁡(NZ/X).

Proof.

Consider the master space W with the action of ℂ×. It has two GIT quotients W⫽Lℂ×=X and W⫽L~=BlZ⁡X. There are three fixed components

Wℂ×=X⊔BlZ⁡X⊔Z,

so we have three continuous Fourier transforms and two conjectural discrete Fourier transforms

The first step of the proof is to show that FTX contains essentially the same information as FTL and that FTBlZ⁡X contains essentially the same information as FTL~. The next step is to prove that

FTL~:QDMℂ×⁡(W)L~→QDM⁡(BlZ⁡X)

is an isomorphism. Then we combine the Fourier transforms to construct Ψ and finally show that Ψ is an isomorphism. □
