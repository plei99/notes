1 Introduction

Representation theory studies some group G with some structure by considering representations (V,ρ:G→GL⁡(V)) where V is a vector space and ρ is a group homomorphism with suitable properties. This seems like a purely algebraic question, but it is already very hard even when G is a finite group (in the nonabelian case). We may now bring in various geometric methods to construct representations of G:

(1)
If G acts on an algebraic variety X, then there is an action of G on the space of functions on X;
(2)
If G acts on an algebraic variety X, then we may consider a G-equivariant line bundle ℒ on X (which is a line bundle on [X/G]), we may consider the cohomology groups Hi(X,ℒ) which are representations of G. If X is projective, then these cohomology groups are finite-dimensional;
(3)
If G acts on X, then it also acts on the cohomology H∙(X) for any generalized cohomology theory;
(4)
There are also more hidden and subtle symmetries, where instead of actual group actions, we consider correspondences acting on cohomology groups. We will see examples of this later when we discuss Springer theory. In addition, we can lift this to the level of derived categories and consider Fourier-Mukai transforms;

1.1 Overview of the course. The first part of the course will focus on the basics of geometric representation theory, largely focusing on Springer theory, the Borel-Weil-Bott theorem, and the geometry of the wonderful compactification G.

The second part of the course will focus on a completely integrable system called the Toda system and its homological mirror symmetry. One motivation for this is the notion of categorification, for example going from the Hecke algebra to the Hecke category, which will turn out to be a category of constructible sheaves.

One origin of categorification in algebraic geometry is Grothendieck’s function-sheaf correspondence, which we will adapt to our setting (in particular, we will not need ℓ-adic sheaves). One particularly simple example of this is upgrading the integers ℤ to the derived category of vector spaces over a field k, where the original integer is recovered as the Euler characteristic.

For a more sophisticated example, we may consider a smooth manifold X with a stratification 𝒮={Sα} by locally closed submanifolds. We may consider the space Fun𝒮(X) of constructible functions (taking values in integers) with respect to 𝒮, which are locally constant on each stratum Sα. The natural categorification of this is the derived category Sh𝒮(X) of constructible sheaves on X, where all cohomology sheaves are locally constant on each stratum Sα. The original constructible function is recovered by taking the Euler characteristic of the stalk at each point. In addition, we may recover the functions as

K0(Sh𝒮(X))≅Fun𝒮(X).

1.2 Homological mirror symmetry. We will now discuss a connection to symplectic geometry. If we have a stratification 𝒮, then we may consider the union

T𝒮∗X≔⨆Sα∈𝒮TSα∗X

of conormal bundles to the strata and consider the microlocal sheaf theory. In particular, each constructible sheaf ℱ has a microlocal stalk at each (x,ξ)∈T𝒮∗X as in Figure 1. For example, if we have a restriction map ℱ(U)→ℱ(V), then the microlocal stalk is

μ(x,ξ)(ℱ)=Cone⁡(ℱ(U)→ℱ(V))[⋯],

where the shift is not canonical. One upshot of this approach is that

K0(Sh𝒮(X))≅Fun𝒮(X)≅HtopBM(T𝒮∗X).

The following theorem inspired many subsequent works.

Theorem 1.1 (Nadler-Zaslow). The dg-category Shc(X) of constructible sheaves on X equivalent the Fukaya category Fuk(T∗X) of the cotangent bundle T∗X of X.

We will now discuss a geometric interpretation. We may consider the characteristic cycle CC⁡(ℱ) of a constructible sheaf, which is a conic Lagrangian cycle in T∗X. However, the objects of the Fukaya category are smooth Lagrangian submanifolds with the cochain complexes of morphisms being given by counting interesection points and holomorphic polygons. See Figure 2 for a visualization. It is usually very difficult to calculate the Fukaya category directly, but the theorem allows us to translate these questions into questions about constructible sheaves, which are more manageable.

Sketch of proof. We will begin by discussing why there is a fully faithful functor Shc⁡(X)→Fuk(T∗X). The first step is to find some nice generators of the constructible sheaf category. For this, we consider open embeddings j:U↪X and extension by zero j!ℂU of the constant sheaf on U. These have characteristic cycles given by the conormal bundle to the boundary ∂U (together with the zero section at U). We will send this sheaf j!ℂU to a smoothing of its characteristic cycle, as depicted in Figure 3.

To prove essential surjectivity, all of the Hom complexes in the Fukaya category can be calculated using Morse theory. For more details, see the original paper by Nadler-Zaslow. □

One reason to consider the Fukaya category is homological mirror symmetry, which was originally proposed by Kontsevich. For certain symplectic manifolds M, there is a mirror partner Mˇ, which is a complex algebraic variety, and Kontsevich conjectured that there is an equivalence of categories

Fuk(M)≃Coh(Mˇ).

The simplest example is when M=T∗S1, where we need to consider the fully wrapped Fukaya category. We have an equivalence

𝒲Fuk(T∗S1)≃Loc(S1)

(modulo technical details), but the upshot is that all characteristic cycles are supported inside the zero section. Local systems are on S1 are simply representations of π1(S1)≅ℤ, which are simply modules over the group algebra ℂ[ℤ]≅ℂ[t,t−1]=𝒪(ℂ×). Ignoring finiteness conditions, these are simply quasicoherent sheaves on ℂ×.

Remark 1.2. For the purposes of representation theory, we will treat π1(S1)=𝕏∙(S1) as cocharacters, which will be identified with the characters 𝕏∙(ℂ×) on the mirror side. This picture generalizes if we consider algebraic tori of any rank and is a version of Langlands duality in the abelian case.

Remark 1.3. Homological mirror symemtry is closely related to the geometric Langlands correspondence, where we send the Hitchin system for G to the character variety for the Langlands dual group G∨. There are some technical issues, but the Betti geometric Langlands correspondence is essentially homological mirror symmetry here.

One modification of this is to complexify the Fukaya side into T∗T, and in this case the (appropriate version of the) wrapped Fukaya category still reduces to local systems on T. Traditionally, we considered T∗Tc (the cotangent bundle of the compact form) because of the SYZ mirror symmetry philosophy, which predicts that a manifold M and its mirror Mˇ have dual Lagrangian torus fibrations. In general, it is very hard to use SYZ to construct mirrors because there are usually singular fibers, which are hard to control.

However, one way to produce a mirror is to believe in homological mirror symmetry and then try to find a variety Mˇ such that

Fuk(M)≃Coh(Mˇ).

If the fibration has a Lagrangian section, then each smooth fiber becomes a Lie group, so Fuk(M) is expected to pick up a “convolution” symmetric monoidal structure, which should correspond to the tensor product of coherent sheaves in some kind of non-linear Fourier transform.

In the second part of the course, we will generalize the duality T∗T↔T∨ to the universal centralizer (or Toda system) JG, where G is a semisimple complex Lie group. This is related to representation theory for two reasons.

  • JG is also called the bi-Whittaker reduction and represents something like the generic part of the representation of G, namely the “commutative part” of the Hecke category;
  • If we add a loop to G, then we obtain the affine Toda system;

  • There is a partial (log) compactification JGlog over the base 𝔤∗⫽G constucted by Balibanu whose fibers are a kind of regular Hessenberg variety (a generalization of Springer fibers). The central fiber is the Peterson variety YP, which is a closed subvariety of the flag variety G/B and is related to the quantum cohomology of G∨/P∨ via the isomorphism

    QH∗(G∨/P∨)≅𝒪(YP).

2 Review of semisimple Lie algebras/groups

Eventually, the groups we are interested in are SLn⁡(ℂ), SOn⁡(ℂ), and Sp2n⁡(ℂ) with Lie algebras 𝔰𝔩n(ℂ), 𝔰𝔬n(ℂ), and 𝔰𝔭2n(ℂ). However, along the way we will encounter groups like Borels and unipotent groups.

2.1 Generalities. Every Lie group has both a left and right action of G on itself given by the multiplication map

m:G×G→G.

We may consider the tangent space 𝔤=TeG at the identity, and left translation of vector fields gives an isomorphism

L:G×𝔤→∼TG(g,ξ)↦Lg∗ξ.

The right action gives another trivialization

R:G×𝔤→∼TG(g,ξ)↦Rg−1∗ξ.

These two are related by the diagram where ι is the map sending an element of G to its inverse. If we fix ξ∈𝔤, then applying L gives us a left-invariant vector field ξℓ on G, and similarly applying R gives a right-invariant vector field ξr.

Lemma 2.1.

(1)
Left (resp. right) invariant vector fields on G are closed under the Lie bracket;
(2)
The left and right-invariant vector fields induce the same Lie bracket on 𝔤.

The main idea of the proof is that the Lie bracket is preserved by diffeomorphisms and in particular left (or right) translation.

Remark 2.2. If we have any action a:G×X→X, then taking the derivative

(da)e:𝔤×X→X

gives an infinitesimal action of G on X (as in a map from 𝔤 to vector fields on X). For example, L is simply

(dm)e,factor 2:G×𝔤→TG

and a similar statement is true for R.

One important action of G on itself is the adjoint action, where G acts on itself by conjugation. On the tangent space, we have (Rg−1)∗Lg∗=Lg∗(Rg−1)∗. By definition, we now have

Adg⁡ξ≔(Rg−1)∗Lg∗ξ,

which induces a map

Ad:G→GL(𝔤).

Therefore, another equivalent definition of the Lie bracket is to simply differentiate the adjoint action. There is also an exponential map exp:𝔤→G, and the Lie bracket is defined as

[ξ,η]≔ddt∫|t=0Adexp⁡(tξ)⁡η.

Remark 2.3. Any finite-dimensional abstract Lie algebra is isomorphic to a subalgebra of 𝔤𝔩n by Ado’s theorem, which says that every finite-dimensional Lie algebra has a finite-dimensional faithful representation. In particular, every finite-dimensional Lie group over ℝ is locally an immersed subgroup of GLn(ℝ) for some n.

We will give some principles of Lie theory:

  • The Lie group is (up to taking its universal cover) determined by its Lie algebra. Given a connected Lie group (H,e), then any discrete covering (H′,e′) inherits a unique group structure whose kernel lies in the center of H′ (this works because the fundamental group of H is abelian due to the group structure). Conversely, any discrete central quotient of H is a Lie group with the same Lie algebra. If we work analytically, H has a universal cover, which is the unique simply connected Lie group with the same Lie algebra as H.

  • If G and H are Lie groups such that G is connected, then any Lie group homomorphism

    ρ:G→H

    is uniquely determined by its derivative dρ:𝔤→𝔥, which is a Lie algebra homomorphism.

  • For 𝔤=Lie⁡G and any embedding 𝔥↪𝔤, the subgroup of G generated by exp⁡(𝔥) is an immersed Lie subgroup of G with Lie algebra 𝔥. For example, if G=S1×S1 and 𝔥 is a line with irrational slope, then H is immersed rather than embedded.

  • Let G and H be finite dimensional Lie groups with G connected and simply connected. Then a map f:TeG→TeH is the differential of a Lie group homomorphism ρ:G→H if and only if it is a Lie algebra homomorphism. The standard trick to prove this is to consider the graph

    𝔤↪𝔤⊕𝔥ξ↦(ξ,f(ξ)),

    which is a Lie algebra embedding. Then we get an immersed subgroup of G×H (this receives a map from G because G is simply connected), which we project down to H.

By Ado’s theorem, we see that the embedding 𝔤↪𝔤𝔩n(ℝ) gives an immersed subgroup G→GLn(ℝ). In addition, the third item actually implies that there is a unique simply connected Lie group for any Lie algebra 𝔤.

Example 2.4. Let X be a projective variety. Then studying the identity component Aut⁡(X)0 is similar to studying

TeAut⁡(X)0=H0(X,TX),

which is a standard result in deformation theory. For example, if X=G/P is a partial flag variety, then H0(X,TX)=𝔤 in almost all cases, but if P=B (the full flag variety), then we really do have

H0(X,TX)=𝔤.

In particular, we have

Aut⁡(G/P)0=G/Z(G),

and when G is semisimple, the center is discrete.

We will call a Lie algebra homomorphism

𝔤→𝔤𝔩(V)

a representation of 𝔤. For example, we previously considered the adjoint representation

ad:𝔤→𝔤𝔩(𝔤).

We can also take the direct sums V⊕W and tensor products V⊗W of representations. It should be noted that the action on V⊗W should take the form

ξ(v⊗w)=ξv⊗w+v⊗ξw

in analogy with differentiating the action of G on V⊗W, which takes the form

g(v⊗w)=gv⊗gw.

2.2 Rough classification of Lie algebras. Given a Lie algebra 𝔤, there is a lower central series

𝔤=D0𝔤⊃D1(𝔤)=[𝔤,𝔤]⊃⋯⊃Dk−1(𝔤)⊃Dk𝔤=[𝔤,Dk−1𝔤]⋯

and the smaller derived series

𝔤=D0𝔤⊃D1𝔤=[𝔤,𝔤]⊃⋯⊃Dk−1𝔤⊃Dk𝔤=[Dk−1𝔤,Dk−1𝔤].

Definition 2.5. A Lie algebra is nilpotent if the lower central series becomes zero at some point.

One example of a nilpotent Lie algbera is if we consider upper-triangular matrices with zeroes on the diagonal (usually denoted 𝔫).

Definition 2.6. A Lie algebra is solvable if the derived series eventually vanishes.

An example of a solvable Lie algebra is the algebra 𝔟 of all upper-triangular matrices. The reason for this is that [𝔟,𝔟]=𝔫. If we write

𝔟=𝔥⊕𝔫,

then we see that [𝔥,𝔥]=0, [𝔥,𝔫]=𝔫, and [𝔫,𝔫]⊂𝔫.

Theorem 2.7 (Engel). Let 𝔤⊂𝔤𝔩(V) be a Lie subalgebra such that every X∈𝔤 is a nilpotent endomorphism of V. Then there exists a nonzero v∈V such that X(v)=0 for all X∈𝔤.

Engel’s theorem implies that all X∈𝔤 can be made (strictly) upper-triangular simultaneously.

Theorem 2.8 (Lie). Let 𝔤⊂𝔤𝔩(V) be a solvable Lie subalgebra over ℂ. Then there is a common eigenvector for all X∈𝔤.

In particular, Lie’s theorem implies that all X∈𝔤 can be simultaneously made upper-triangular.

We will now consider all solvable ideals in 𝔤. In fact, if we have a finite collection of solvable ideals, then the sum is still solvable, so there exists a maximal solvable ideal of 𝔤, called the radical of 𝔤 and denoted Rad⁡(𝔤). This fits into the exact sequence

0→Rad⁡(𝔤)→𝔤→𝔤/Rad⁡(𝔤)→0.

2.3 Semisimple and reductive Lie algebras.

Definition 2.9. A Lie algebra 𝔤 is semisimple if Rad⁡(𝔤)=0.

Example 2.10. The Lie algebra 𝔤/Rad⁡(𝔤) is semisimple.

Definition 2.11. A Lie algebra is reductive if Rad⁡(𝔤)=Z(𝔤).

Remark 2.12. There are other characterizations of reductive Lie algebras, for example that the nilradical (maximal nilpotent ideal) Rnil(𝔤), which fits into the exact sequence

0→Rnil(𝔤)→𝔤→𝔤/Rnil(𝔤)→0,

vanishes.

We now give some important properties of semisimple Lie algebras.

Theorem 2.13. Let 𝔤 be a semisimple Lie algebra. Then every finite-dimensional representation of 𝔤 decomposes uniquely as a direct sum of irreducible representations.

Sketch of proof. There are two ways to do this, either algebraically (which is tedious) or by using Weyl’s unitary trick. The key fact using the unitary trick is that every complex semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 has a unique compact real form 𝔤c satisfying 𝔤c⊗ℝℂ=𝔤 (being a real form) and being the Lie algebra of a simply connected compact Lie group.

For example, the compact real form of 𝔰𝔩n(ℂ) is 𝔰𝔲n (traceles anti-Hermitian matrices), which corresponds to SUn⊂SLn⁡(ℂ). The unitary trick is to restrict to the action of the compact form Gc on V and then try to find a Gc-invariant Hermitian metric on V, which will split W⊂V for free. The way to find the inner product is to pick any Hermitian metric ⟨−,−⟩ and a Haar measure μ on Gc such that

∫Gcdμ=1,

and define a new inner product by

⟨v,w⟩Gc≔∫Gc⟨gv,gw⟩dμ(g)

(which is the same trick we use for finite groups). Finally, the Gc-representations lift to G, so we are done. □

Theorem 2.14. In fact, a Lie algebra is semisimple if and only if all of its finite-dimensional representations are semisimple.

Proof. Consider the adjoint representation ad:𝔤→𝔤𝔩(𝔤). Because it is semisimple, we have a decomposition

𝔤=Rad⁡(𝔤)⊕𝔤/Rad⁡(𝔤).

In particular, we must have [Rad⁡(𝔤),𝔤ss]=0, where 𝔤ss=𝔤/Rad⁡(𝔤).

If Rad⁡(g)≠0, then by Lie’s theorem says that any representation must have an eigenvector, so all irreducible representations must be 1-dimensional. This implies that

𝔤=ℓ1⊕⋯⊕ℓk⊕𝔤ss=Z(𝔤)⊕𝔤ss.

An abelian Lie algebra has indecomposable representations which are not semisimple, for example representing a line by matrices of the form [0∗00], so the radical must have vanished to begin with. □

Remark 2.15. Levi’s theorem actually states that 𝔤=Rad⁡(𝔤)⋊𝔤ss.

Theorem 2.16 (Preservation of Jordan decomposition). Let 𝔤 be semisimple. For any X∈𝔤, there exist Xs,Xn∈𝔤 such that Xs+Xn=X and for any finite-dimensional representation

ρ:𝔤→𝔤𝔩(V),

the Jordan normal form of ρ(X) has ρ(X)s=ρ(Xs) and ρ(X)n=ρ(Xn).

Remark 2.17. If we consider the adjoint representation of 𝔤 (which is faithful), then in fact the condition on the Jordan decomposition in any representation actually implies that X=Xs+Xn.

Example 2.18. If we consider 𝔤=ℂ, then the representation

t↦[tt0t]

has ρ(X)s and ρ(X)n not in the image of ρ in general.

Definition 2.19. If 𝔤 is semisimple, then we say X∈𝔤 is semisimple if X=Xs and nilpotent if X=Xn.

We will now consider reductive Lie algebras. In fact, by Levi’s theorem and the fact that [Z(𝔤),𝔤ss]=0, 𝔤 is reductive if and only if it is the product of a semisimple and an abelian Lie algebra, which is equivalent to 𝔤=Z(𝔤)⊕[𝔤,𝔤].

Definition 2.20. A Lie group is semisimple if its Lie algebra is semisimple.

We will now consider all complex semisimple Lie groups, which are actually affine algebraic groups. By Borel’s theorem, they are actually all linear, meaning closed subgroups of some GLn. If 𝔤 is semisimple, then there is a simply connected form Gsc and a tower down to the adjoint form Gsc=Gsr/Z(Gsc).

Example 2.21. For the Lie algebra 𝔰𝔩n, then the simply connected form is SLn and the adjoint form is PGLn.

Example 2.22. If we consider 𝔰𝔭2n, then the simply connected form is Sp2n(ℂ) and the adjoint form is PSp2n(ℂ).

Example 2.23. For SOn, there is a form SOn, which for n≥3 has fundamental group ℤ/2, so the simply connected form is the universal cover Spinn and the adjoint form is PSOn.

Remark 2.24. We may also consider simple Lie algebras, which are semisimple Lie algebras with no nontrivial ideals. Then the classical simple Lie algebras are the ones we have already listed and are the only infinite families.

There is also the Cartan-Killing criterion of semisimple Lie algebras.

Definition 2.25. An invariant bilinear form on 𝔤 is given by

B:𝔤×𝔤→ℂ

satisfying the condition that

adx⁡(B(y,z))≔B([x,y],z)+B(y,[x,z])=0.

Definition 2.26. The Killing form is the form

B(x,y)=Tr⁡(adx∘ady).

Because the trace has cyclic invariance and the trace is invariant under conjugation, this is invariant and symmetric.

Theorem 2.27 (Cartan-Killing criterion). A Lie algebra 𝔤 is semisimple if and only if the Killing form is nondegenerate.

In particular, the means that the adjoint and coadjoint representations are isomorphic.

Remark 2.28. If Z(𝔤)≠0, then adx=0 for all x∈Z(𝔤), so B(x,−)=0 for B the Killing form.

We now briefly pause to consider issues of analytic vs algebraic categories. If we are considering semisimple Lie groups over ℂ, then they are in fact also (linear) algebraic groups. However, if we leave the semisimple setting, then there are differences. For example, if we consider 𝔤=ℂ, then the algebraic options are (ℂ,+)=𝔾a, (ℂ×,×)=𝔾m, and compact 1-dimensional tori, namely elliptic curves. Usually, in algebraic groups theory, we will only consider affine algebraic groups.

Theorem 2.29 (Borel). Every affine algebraic group G is linear, and has a closed immersion to some GL(V).

In the analytic world, there is the map

exp:ℂ→ℂ×,

which is not algebraic. In particular, 𝔾a and 𝔾m are totally different and have no nontrivial morphisms between them (and we have to consider their formal completions to recover the exponential map). In fact, the category Rep(𝔾m) is semisimple (prove this using the unitary trick for S1), while the same is not true for 𝔾a (consider the representation by matrices of the form [1∗01]). However, any analytic Lie group over ℂ not containing a compact torus is algebraic.

We now proceed to defining reductive groups. Recall that we essentially need to figure out what to do with the center Z(𝔤) of a reductive Lie algebra 𝔤. In the algebraic group world, we will consider tori, which are groups isomorphic to products of copies of 𝔾m.

Definition 2.30. A reductive group G is a finite quotient of the product of an (affine) algebraic torus and a semisimple algebraic group.

In particular, we have

G=Z(G)×Z(Gder)Gder,

where Gder=[G,G] is semisimple and Z(G) might be disconnected. For example, Z(SLn)=μn, which is clearly not connected. Alternatively, there is a quotient morphism

G→G/Z(Gder)=Z(G)/Z(Gder)×Gad,

where again Gad=G/Z(G). Here, we see that Z(G)/Z(Gder) is always connected. Using Langlands duality, we can produce a cover by something with connected center. A third alternative is to rewrite the product as

G=Z(G)0×Z(Gder)0Gder,

where Z(Gder)0=Z(G)0∩Gder.

Example 2.31. In the case where G=GLn, we have Z(GLn)=ℂ× and (GLn)der=SLn, so we have

GLn=ℂ××μnSLn.

Reductive groups are characterized by complete reducibility of their finite- dimensional representations (in characteristic zero). In addition, algebraic and analytic morphisms between reductive groups agree, so we no longer need to worry about the difference between algebraic and analytic categories. Representations of reductive groups also satisfy the preservation of Jordan decompositions, namely that for any g∈G, there exist gs,gu∈G such that g=gsgu=gugs and for any finite-dimensional representation

ρ:G→GL(V),

the Jordan decomposition of ρ(g) satisfies ρ(g)s=ρ(gs) and ρ(g)u=ρ(gu).

Lemma 2.32. If 𝔤 is semisimple, then any invariant bilinear form is symmetric. In addition, any invariant non-degenerate bilinear form is a scalar multiple of the Killing form.

Proof. Any invariant bilinear form B induces a map

𝔤→𝔤∗

as morphisms of G≔Gad-representations. Recall that 𝔤 decomposes into a product

𝔤=𝔤1×⋯×𝔤k

of simple Lie algebras, so we have a morphism

𝔤1×⋯×𝔤k→𝔤1∗×⋯×𝔤k∗.

Using the Killing form, we have an isomorphism 𝔤i≅𝔤i∗, so by Schur’s lemma, the map 𝔤i→𝔤j∗ is zero if i≠j and a scalar multiple of the identity if i=j. Therefore, any invariant bilinear form is a sum of scalar multiples of Killing forms on each simple factor, which are all symmetric. The second statement follows similarly. □

2.4 Cartan subalgebras. We will begin by considering general 𝔤 and specialize to semisimple ones later.

Definition 2.33. If 𝔞⊂𝔤 is a Lie subalgebra, then the normalizer of 𝔞 in 𝔤 is given by

𝔫𝔤(𝔞)={x∈𝔤:[x,𝔞]⊂𝔞}.

Definition 2.34. A subalgebra 𝔥⊂𝔤 is a Cartan subalgebra if

(1)
𝔥 is nilpotent;
(2)
𝔥=𝔫𝔤(𝔥) (as in it is equal to its own normalizer).

We will see later that every Lie algebra has a Cartan subalgebra.

For any x∈𝔤, let

Px(T)=det⁡(TId−adx)=∑i=0nai(x)Ti

denote the characteristic polynomial of adx. If we choose coordinates x1,…,xn on 𝔤, then each ai(x) is a homogeneous polynomial in x1,…,xn of degree n−i.

Definition 2.35. The rank of 𝔤 is the least i such that ai(x) is not identically zero.

Example 2.36. If 𝔤≅ℂn is abelian, then Px(T)=Tn for all x, so 𝔤 has rank n.

Remark 2.37. Note that i<n in the nonabelian case because an(x)=1 is never zero and i>0 because x∈ker⁡(adx) for all x and therefore a0(x)=0 for all x.

From now on, we will denote the rank of 𝔤 by r.

Definition 2.38. We say that x∈𝔤 is strongly regular if ar(x)≠0.

Remark 2.39. We will see another notion of regularity later, so we call this strongly regular to distinguish it.

We will denote the set of strongly regular elements by 𝔤sreg. The definition of a strongly regular element implies the following.

Proposition 2.40. The set 𝔤sreg is a connected open dense subset of 𝔤 (in the Zariski topology and hence also the analytic topology).

Theorem 2.41. If x∈𝔤sreg, then the generalized eigenspace of 0 for adx, denoted 𝔤x0, is a Cartan subalgebra of 𝔤.

Note that by definition, we have dim⁡𝔤x0=r, so the dimension of this Cartan equals the rank of 𝔤. In fact, we have already described all Cartan subalgebras.

Theorem 2.42. Let 𝔤 be a Lie algebra over ℂ. Then all Cartan subalgebras of 𝔤 are of the form 𝔤x0 for some x∈𝔤sreg.

These Cartan subalgebras are all conjugate under the action of

Ginn=⟨eadx∣x∈𝔤⟩↪Aut⁡(𝔤),

so in particular Ginn acts transitively on the set of Cartan subalgebras.

Remark 2.43. If G is reductive, then Ginn=Gad=G/Z(G).

2.5 Root systems. We will now specialize to the semisimple case, which is much more explicit.

Theorem 2.44. Let 𝔥 be a Cartan subalgebra of a semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤. Then

(1)
𝔥 is abelian;
(2)
𝔥 is its own centralizer, i.e. Z𝔤(𝔥)=𝔥;
(3)
Every element of 𝔥 is semisimple.
(4)

𝔤=𝔥⊕⨁α∈Φ𝔤α,

[x,y]=[limn→∞xn,limn→∞yn]=limn→∞[xn,yn]=0.

[y,z]=[limn→∞yn,limn→∞zn]=limn→∞[yn,zn]=0.
□

2.8 Flag varieties and the Bruhat decomposition. We will now discuss Borel subalgebras 𝔟⊂𝔤.

Definition 2.66. A Borel subalgebra in 𝔤 is a maximal solvable subalgebra. A Borel subgroup in G is a maximal connected solvable subgroup.

Note that if we have a Borel subalgebra, we can integrate it to get a Borel subgroup B⊂G. Here are some properties:

(1)
All Borel subalgebras are conjugate under the adjoint representation;
(2)
If 𝔟 is a Borel subalgebra, then 𝔫𝔤(𝔟)=𝔟 and NG(𝔟)=B;
(3)
Every Borel subalgebra contains a Cartan subalgebra. On the other hand, a fixed Cartan is contained in |WT|-many Borel subalgebras, which form a WT-torsor. In fact, they are in bijection with the set of choices of positive roots via
Φ+↦𝔟=𝔥⊕⨁α∈Φ+𝔤α.

For example, we can let 𝔥 be the diagonal matrices in 𝔰𝔩n, and we can generate all Borels by taking the upper-triangular matrices as a base point and conjugating by permutation matrices.

(4)
If h∈𝔟 is regular semisimple, then in fact 𝔥≔Z𝔤(h)⊂𝔟.

Now let ℬ be the set of Borel subalgebras of 𝔤. We can give it the structure of an algebraic variety as follows. First, consider the Grassmannian

Gr⁡(dim⁡𝔟=r+#Φ+,𝔤),

which is naturally an algebraic variety which carries a G-action induced from the adjoint representation. Then ℬ sits inside as a subset which is in fact the orbit of any Borel 𝔟 under this action. This gives

ℬ=G⋅𝔟≅G/NG(𝔟)≅G/B.

It is easy to check that ℬ is in fact closed, so it is a projective variety called the flag variety of G (and the quotient description means it is a homogeneous space).

Remark 2.67. Because the Borel subgroup B always contains the center Z(G), the flag variety is insensitive to the choice of intermediate form of G. If G is reductive, then this construction kills the center, so we only see the semisimple part of G in the flag variety.

Example 2.68. If G=SLn (or PGLn), then in fact

ℬ={0⊊V1⊊V2⊊⋯⊊Vn=ℂn∣dim⁡Vi=i}

is the usual flag variety of ℂn. This clearly admits a GLn-equivariant surjection from the variety of frames

{{v1,…,vn} is a basis of ℂn}≅GLn}.

If we consider the standard flag

⟨e1⟩⊂⟨e1,e2⟩⊂⋯⊂⟨e1,…,en⟩=ℂn,

then the stabilizer of this flag is the standard Borel subgroup B+ of upper-triangular matrices, so we see that ℬ=G/B+.

Now we may restrict the G-action on ℬ to the Borel B, where we choose a particular Borel B as a base point. Then the set of B-orbits is denoted by

{B-orbits in ℬ}≅B∖G/B.

We may also consider the G×G-action on ℬ×ℬ and restrict to the diagonal subgroup GΔ. Then the set of GΔ-orbits is given by

B∖G/B→∼{GΔ-orbits in ℬ×ℬ}B⋅𝔟′↦GΔ⋅(𝔟,𝔟′).

To check that this is well-defined, suppose that 𝔟″=b⋅𝔟′. This follows from the fact that

GΔ⋅(𝔟,𝔟″)=GΔ⋅(b⋅(𝔟,𝔟′))=GΔ⋅(𝔟,𝔟′).

To check surjectivity, we look at the orbit of (𝔟1,𝔟2), but we can bring 𝔟1 to 𝔟, and surjectivity follows. To check injectivity, we see that if GΔ⋅(𝔟,𝔟′)=GΔ(𝔟,𝔟″), then (𝔟,𝔟″)=g⋅(𝔟,𝔟′) for some g∈G. But if g⋅𝔟=𝔟, then g∈B and thus 𝔟″∈B⋅𝔟′.

We will now consider a more refined bijection which will give the Bruhat decomposition. Fix a maximal torus T⊂B, or equivalently a Cartan subalgebra 𝔥⊂𝔟. Then we have a map

WT→B∖G/B,w˙↦Bw˙⋅B,

where we choose a representative w˙∈wT of a coset in WT=NG(T)/T. Because T⋅B=B, this map is well-defined. Taking stock, we now have maps
